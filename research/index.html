Computational Catalysis

My current work focuses on developing accurate and efficient density functional theory (DFT) methods for modeling catalytic and electrochemical surfaces. Real surfaces are semi-infinite: a finite slab of atoms sitting on top of an effectively infinite bulk material, but standard DFT calculations require truncating this bulk into a finite, periodic simulation cell, which can introduce artificial finite-size effects and spurious interactions between periodic images. To address this, I am developing a bulk boundary condition formalism that exploits the nearsightedness of electronic interactions, confining the expensive part of the calculation to a localized surface region while rigorously imposing bulk electronic and electrostatic behavior in the interior. This approach enables accurate, efficient prediction of surface and adsorption energies relevant to catalytic reactions.

Electronic Structure Methods

Alongside applying DFT to specific materials problems, I develop and extend the underlying numerical methods that make large-scale, real-space electronic structure calculations possible, primarily within the SPARC electronic structure code. This includes a real-space formulation of Hubbard-corrected DFT (DFT+U), where I derived expressions for the energy, atomic forces, and stress tensor compatible with finite-difference discretization and built a large-scale parallel implementation that outperforms established plane-wave codes by more than an order of magnitude in time to solution. To support this and other DFT+U calculations, I developed a spectral solver for isolated atoms that achieves sub-microhartree eigenvalue accuracy, used to generate exchange-correlation-consistent atomic orbital projectors. My bulk boundary condition formalism for semi-infinite surfaces, described above, is also fundamentally a methods contribution: a new way of imposing boundary conditions in real-space DFT that keeps large surface calculations tractable.

Defects in Materials

Before focusing on surface and method development, my research centered on understanding how defects form and evolve in materials, from bulk metals to two-dimensional semiconductors. In aluminum, I used DFT calculations to study how hydrostatic strain drives the clustering of vacancies, showing that compressive strain is a key factor in the collapse of vacancy clusters into prismatic dislocation loops on the (111) plane, consistent with experimental observations. In two-dimensional molybdenum disulfide (MoS2), I investigated how nanopores nucleate and grow during synthesis, combining DFT-calculated etching energies with Marcus theory, kinetic Monte Carlo simulations, and chemical graph theory. This work traced the atomic-scale etching mechanisms responsible for pore nucleation and showed how the surrounding chemical environment can be tuned to control pore geometry and size, from irregular shapes to nearly perfect triangles, offering a route to engineer nanopores for applications like desalination and DNA sequencing.
